If the dashboard is what you care about most, Monarch Money is the better fit. It brings connected accounts, budgets, net worth, investments, recurring transactions, and reports into a polished consumer interface.

Expense Budget Tracker becomes the more interesting **Monarch Money alternative** when you care about the system underneath the dashboard: an inspectable ledger, native-currency transactions, transfers that remain transfers, shared workspaces, a SQL API, agent automation, and the option to self-host.

That is the decision in plain English. Monarch gives you a smoother all-in-one overview. Expense Budget Tracker gives technical users and multi-currency households more control over how financial data enters the system, how it is modeled, and where it runs.

## The dashboard question comes first

People searching for a **Monarch Money dashboard alternative** usually aren't asking for another transaction list. They want one place to answer a handful of recurring questions:

- How much did we spend this month?
- Are we over budget anywhere?
- What is our net worth now?
- Which account changed?
- Is a recurring bill about to land?

Monarch is built for that overview. Its [dashboard uses removable and reorderable widgets](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360058127551-Customizing-Your-Dashboard), including budget, net worth, recurring transactions, spending trends, transactions, investments, and other summaries. Web and mobile layouts can be arranged separately. If you want to open an app and scan a broad financial picture without designing the system yourself, this is a real strength.

Expense Budget Tracker's dashboard is narrower. It shows spending breakdowns, balances over time, and the impact of currency movements. The budget grid and ledger do more of the day-to-day work. That is less of a ready-made financial home screen, but it is useful when you want to move from a chart back to the accounts and transactions that produced it.

For a **Monarch Money budgeting dashboard**, Monarch has the broader ready-made interface. Expense Budget Tracker makes more sense when the dashboard must sit on top of a data model you can inspect and query.

## The budgeting models solve different problems

Monarch supports [Category and Flex budgeting](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048883631-Understanding-Your-Budget-in-Monarch). Category budgeting assigns a plan to individual categories. Flex budgeting, which Monarch uses as its default, groups less predictable spending into a higher-level flexible bucket. Both use a monthly cash-flow model: expected income funds expenses and savings.

That setup works well when you want the app to guide the monthly plan. Monarch uses the previous six months of category spending to fill the first budget, then lets you change the amounts. Its web budget also shows Budget, Actual, and Remaining columns, so this is not a choice between explicit and hidden numbers. The meaningful difference is how those numbers are organized.

Expense Budget Tracker takes a plainer route. Its monthly grid puts planned, actual, and gap values next to each other, with an append-only audit trail for budget changes. The plan remains close to the ledger. This is a good fit if you already know how you want to budget and mainly need the system to show where reality diverged from the plan.

Pick the behavior you want from the software:

- Choose Monarch if you want Category or Flex budgeting, a calculated starting plan, and optional rollovers.
- Choose Expense Budget Tracker if you want a consistent planned-versus-actual grid, ledger-backed actuals, and a history of budget changes.

## Net worth: polished reporting or ledger traceability

A **Monarch Money net worth dashboard** combines account aggregation with a dedicated net worth view. Monarch also includes spending, cash-flow, and investment reporting, so it can serve as the household's broad financial overview rather than only a budget tool.

Its newer long-range planning goes further. [Forecasting in Monarch](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/48344305092244-Forecasting-in-Monarch) projects future net worth and cash flow from account data, spending history, assumptions, and life events. Forecasting belongs to the Monarch Plus tier. Because plan details can change, use Monarch's [current pricing page](https://www.monarch.com/pricing) rather than a comparison article quoting a stale amount.

Expense Budget Tracker does not try to reproduce that whole planning interface. Its balance model starts lower down: running account balances are derived from ledger entries, transfers between your own accounts are first-class records, and totals can be viewed in a reporting currency. Balance-over-time charts then show how the combined picture changed.

That tradeoff is easy to miss. Monarch gives you a richer net worth product out of the box. Expense Budget Tracker gives you a shorter path from the reported balance back to the underlying ledger. If the dedicated net worth experience is the main reason you are shopping, read the more focused [open-source net worth tracker comparison](/blog/net-worth-tracker-open-source-spreadsheet-alternative/).

## Imports are not the same as reconciliation

Monarch works best as a connected-account product. Its [getting-started guide](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048393272-Getting-Started-with-Monarch) recommends linking accounts so balances and transactions arrive through data providers. Monarch also supports [CSV transaction imports](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/4409682789908-Import-Transaction-Data-Manually-from-Banks-or-Other-Finance-Apps) into synced or manual accounts, and separate balance-history imports can fill historical gaps.

The distinction between importing and reconciling matters. Monarch lets you create transactions manually, but its [manual-transaction documentation](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360058441811-Creating-Manual-Transactions) says there is no reconciliation feature. If a matching bank transaction syncs later, Monarch does not automatically remove the manual entry. A manual transaction added to a synced account also does not change that account's balance.

That is not a reason to dismiss Monarch. It is a reason to test your actual workflow before moving years of history. Connected feeds are convenient, while CSVs and manual entries still need review.

Expense Budget Tracker has no automatic bank syncing. Data comes in through manual entry or a deliberate import workflow. Its agent can inspect a bank statement or card export, use the workspace's accounts and categories, write transactions through the API, and help compare the resulting balance with the statement. You remain responsible for reviewing what it writes.

This asks more of you than passive sync, but it makes the boundary clear. Nothing appears merely because a connection fetched it. If that is what you want, see the full guide to [importing bank statements into an expense tracker](/blog/how-to-import-bank-statements-into-an-expense-tracker/) or the comparison of [budget apps without bank linking](/blog/budget-app-without-bank-linking/).

## Household sharing in Monarch uses one shared view

Monarch has a clear household model. According to its [getting-started guide](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048393272-Getting-Started-with-Monarch), each household member gets a separate login, but everyone shares the same accounts, transactions, and budget. All members have full visibility; specific accounts cannot be hidden from one household member.

That is convenient for couples who want one complete household picture. There is no ambiguity about whether one partner is looking at an incomplete budget. Ownership filters can help separate personal and shared activity inside that common dataset, but they do not create private account access.

Expense Budget Tracker organizes collaboration around workspaces. A user can have a personal workspace, join a shared one, or create another workspace for a separate context. Within the workspace, the ledger, budgets, and dashboards stay together. This model is useful when “household” is not the only boundary you need—for example, when personal and shared finances should not live in the same dataset.

Neither model replaces a conversation about access. Monarch makes full household visibility the default. Expense Budget Tracker makes the workspace the boundary. For a comparison centered on shared planning, see [the best budget apps for couples](/blog/best-budget-app-for-couples/).

## Multi-currency support exposes the biggest structural difference

This is the clearest reason to choose one product over the other.

Monarch's official [currency documentation](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048393552-International-Accounts-and-Currency) says the product does not distinguish currencies behind the scenes or perform currency conversion. Combining accounts denominated in different currencies can therefore produce misleading totals. A USD 10,000 account and a CAD 10,000 account can appear as a combined “$20,000” even though the values are not equivalent.

Expense Budget Tracker was designed around a different model:

1. Each account has its native currency.
2. Each transaction keeps the currency and amount in which it happened.
3. Exchange rates are applied when the data is read for reporting.
4. FX impact can be inspected instead of disappearing into a rewritten historical amount.

That order matters for a household with a salary in USD, rent in EUR, and savings in GBP. The ledger preserves what happened; the dashboard converts it for the current question. Transfers between Expense Budget Tracker accounts are first-class ledger records. Monarch handles its own transfers differently, using transfer categories that are excluded from budgets and cash flow.

If several currencies are part of normal life, Expense Budget Tracker is the more suitable **multi-currency budget app**. The detailed reasoning is in [Multi-Currency Budgeting for Expats](/blog/multi-currency-budgeting-for-expats/).

## Data control changes what “automation” means

Monarch automates the consumer workflow: connect institutions, receive transactions, categorize them, and review a prepared dashboard. That is the automation most people want, and it removes a lot of routine work.

Expense Budget Tracker automates through an inspectable machine interface. It provides:

- a restricted SQL API protected by workspace-level row-level security
- a discovery flow that lets Claude Code, Codex, OpenClaw, or another capable agent create its own connection after email verification
- AI chat over the user's financial data
- published schema and API specifications
- Docker Compose self-hosting with Postgres

This makes it an **open source Monarch Money alternative** for people who want scripts, agents, or their own infrastructure to participate in the workflow. The [source is available on GitHub](https://github.com/kirill-markin/expense-budget-tracker). It is also a more demanding choice. API keys must be stored carefully, agent-written changes need review, and self-hosting means operating the service yourself.

You do not need to self-host to use it. The [managed app](https://app.expense-budget-tracker.com/) is the fastest start, while the [getting-started guide](/docs/getting-started/) covers both hosted and self-hosted routes. The [features page](/features/) shows the current product scope.

## Who should stay with Monarch Money?

Stay with Monarch if most of these sound right:

- You want connected bank feeds to be the normal way transactions arrive.
- A polished web and mobile dashboard matters more than direct access to the data model.
- You want budgeting, goals, recurring transactions, investments, and net worth in one consumer app.
- Your household is comfortable with every member seeing every connected account.
- Your finances fit Monarch's currency model.
- You want the product vendor to operate the entire system.

Switching tools creates work. If Monarch already gives you a dashboard you trust and you do not need multi-currency conversion, API access, or self-hosting, staying is a sensible decision.

## Who should switch to Expense Budget Tracker?

Expense Budget Tracker is a better fit if several of these are non-negotiable:

- You want an inspectable ledger behind the charts.
- You prefer manual or agent-assisted statement imports over permanent bank connections.
- Transfers must remain first-class events in the balance model.
- You budget with explicit planned, actual, and gap values.
- Multiple native currencies need to coexist without being flattened into one amount.
- You want to query or update the data through a documented SQL API.
- You need shared workspaces or a **self-hosted Monarch Money alternative**.

The tradeoff is real: you give up part of Monarch's polished, broad dashboard experience and take more responsibility for data entry, review, and possibly infrastructure. In return, the system becomes more inspectable and programmable.

## A low-risk way to decide

Do not migrate everything first. Test the part of your finances most likely to reveal a mismatch.

1. Pick one checking or card account with a normal month of activity.
2. Export a CSV statement from the bank.
3. Add the account, its native currency, and a small category set in Expense Budget Tracker.
4. Connect an agent through the documented onboarding flow, or enter a small sample manually.
5. Check the imported categories and transfers, then add the rest of the month if the sample looks right.
6. Compare the closing balance, budget actuals, and dashboard totals with the bank and with Monarch.

This test tells you more than a feature list. You will see whether deliberate imports feel reassuring or tedious, whether the budget grid fits your planning style, and whether ledger access is genuinely useful to you.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the closest open-source alternative to Monarch Money?

Expense Budget Tracker covers budgeting, balances, dashboards, shared workspaces, multi-currency reporting, AI chat, an API, and self-hosting. It does not copy Monarch's interface or connected-account workflow. Choose it for ledger and infrastructure control, not as a pixel-for-pixel Monarch clone.

### Does Expense Budget Tracker automatically sync bank accounts?

No. It supports manual entry and statement-import workflows through connected agents, but it does not claim automatic bank syncing. That difference should be part of the decision, not hidden in the small print.

### Can Monarch Money import CSV files?

Yes. Monarch supports CSV imports for transactions and account-balance history. File structure, required fields, duplicate handling, and account assignment still matter, so review the import rather than assuming that upload and reconciliation are the same thing.

### Which option is better for net worth tracking?

Choose Monarch if you want a dedicated net worth dashboard, investment context, and optional long-range forecasting in one managed interface. Choose Expense Budget Tracker if you want account balances derived from an inspectable ledger and reporting across multiple currencies without flattening their underlying amounts.

### Can couples use both products?

Yes, but the sharing models differ. Monarch gives household members separate logins with full visibility into one shared financial picture and one budget. Expense Budget Tracker uses workspaces, which can separate personal and shared contexts.

## Which should you choose?

Choose Monarch if you want a polished, connected dashboard that already covers budgets, net worth, investments, recurring transactions, and reports. You may not need to switch at all.

Choose Expense Budget Tracker when native-currency data, ledger-derived balances, controlled imports, programmable access, or self-hosting matter more. It does not try to copy Monarch's breadth. It gives you direct access to more of the financial machinery.

If that tradeoff matches what you need, [open Expense Budget Tracker](https://app.expense-budget-tracker.com/) and test it with one real account before deciding whether to move the rest.
